Time runs slower near the black hole

Gravity does not only bend light; it slows time. A clock close to the hole ticks slower than your clock far away. Anyone sitting next to it would feel nothing odd, since their seconds feel normal to them, but compared with you their clock keeps falling behind. Disk material is also orbiting very fast, which slows its clocks a little more.

Seconds are too small to show the gap building up, so use the time-lapse to speed up every clock and watch minutes, days and years pile up. Drag the probe in the side view, or use the slider, to move it closer to the hole.

Measured in event-horizon radii (rs). The slider is logarithmic, so the left end reaches to within a ten-billionth of the horizon.

A clock hovering at distance r runs at √(1 − rs/r) of your rate. A clock orbiting with the disk runs at √(1 − 3rs/2r), because its speed adds to the slowdown. Months are 1/12 of a year (30.44 days). The time-lapse speeds up the clocks only; the disk and photons keep moving at a watchable pace. In the side view, photons also move more slowly near the hole when timed by your clock (the Shapiro delay).

Interstellar, explained

Why Gargantua looks like this

The black hole in Christopher Nolan’s Interstellar (2014), Gargantua, was rendered by the visual effects company Double Negative from equations worked out with physicist Kip Thorne, the film’s scientific adviser. Its famous halo over the top and thin arch underneath are the same effect as in the picture above: light from the far side of the disk, bent over and under the hole. Set the viewing angle to a few degrees for the film’s view. The film left out Doppler brightening, which makes the side of the disk coming towards you far brighter; tick Doppler brightening to see what a real one would look like.

Why an hour on Miller’s planet costs seven years

Miller’s planet orbits so close to Gargantua that one hour there equals seven years on Earth, a slowdown of about 61,000 times. The landing party spends a little over three hours on the surface; back on the Endurance, Romilly waits 23 years, 4 months and 8 days. Press Miller’s planet above to recreate it: at one year per second, your clock races through 23 years while the probe’s clock creeps through about three hours.

Could that really happen?

Not around a black hole like the one on this page. Around a non-spinning black hole, stable orbits end at 3 horizon radii, where clocks run at 71% of your rate: an hour there costs only 1 hour 25 minutes. To lose seven years per hour you would have to hover, rockets firing, about 80 metres above the horizon of a black hole as heavy as Gargantua (100 million times the mass of the Sun). Gargantua manages it because it spins at almost the maximum rate physics allows, which lets stable orbits sit right next to the horizon.

Key to the light paths

  • Direct. Near side of the disk, almost a straight line to you.
  • Bent over the top. Far side of the disk, top surface. This is the arch above the hole.
  • Bent underneath. Far side of the disk, bottom surface. This is the thinner arch below.
  • Wrapped around. Light that circled the hole before escaping: the thin photon ring.
  • Falls in. Look in these directions and nothing comes back. That is the black shadow.
  • Time rings. How fast a clock hovering there ticks, compared with yours far away.

Things to try

  1. Drag gravity down to 0%. The disk becomes a plain flat ellipse, the hole shrinks to a small dot and the arches vanish. Bring it back up slowly and watch the far side of the disk lift up over the top of the hole.
  2. Press Sweep the angle. Near edge-on you get the familiar arch shape; looking down from above it becomes a ring. Every angle shows the far side wrapped around, so there is no special front.
  3. Turn on Colour the picture by light path to match each region of the picture to its path in the side view.
  4. Turn on Doppler brightening. The disk orbits at about half the speed of light near its inner edge, so the side coming towards you is much brighter.
  5. Drag the probe clock in the side view towards the hole. At the photon sphere it runs at 58% of your speed; at the event horizon it stops completely, as seen from where you are.
  6. Press Miller’s planet (Interstellar) and watch 23 years pass on your clock while the probe ages about three hours.
