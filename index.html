The timeline stretches as it goes: seconds on the left, decades on the right, and each band is a bigger unit. Every equal step is 10× more time, so the gap between two clocks never changes: that gap is the slowdown.

The maths behind the clocks

A clock hovering at distance r runs at √(1 − rs/r) of your rate, where rs is the event horizon’s radius. A clock orbiting with the disk runs at √(1 − 3rs/2r), because its speed adds to the slowdown. Months are 1/12 of a year (30.44 days). The time-lapse speeds up the clocks only; the disk and photons keep moving at a watchable pace. In the side view, photons also move more slowly near the hole when timed by your clock (the Shapiro delay).

Why an hour costs seven years

Miller’s planet sits so deep in Gargantua’s gravity that its clocks run 61,000 times slower. Romilly waits 23 years while the crew ages three hours.

Could that really happen?

Only around a black hole spinning almost as fast as physics allows, as Gargantua does. A non-spinning one can’t hold an orbit that deep.

Could you survive a trip to Miller’s planet?

Follow the Endurance crew from arrival to the planet. Each leg of the trip has its own danger, and gravity itself is not the one you’d expect.

Leg 1 of 4

Catch the planet

Miller’s planet races around Gargantua at a big fraction of light speed: far too fast to catch with rockets. So the Endurance borrows speed from gravity, swinging past a massive object to change speed without fuel.

  1. The closer an orbit, the faster it must go. Miller’s planet orbits so close to Gargantua that it moves incredibly fast, and the Endurance has to match that speed to reach it.
  2. Rockets can’t manage it. Every extra bit of speed needs fuel to push the ship and all the fuel not yet burnt, so the fuel needed grows exponentially.
  3. A slingshot borrows speed instead. The ship falls towards a moving massive object and climbs away again. Measured from that object, it leaves as fast as it arrived, only in a new direction.
  4. That object is itself moving around Gargantua, so the new direction adds to (or takes away from) the ship’s speed around Gargantua. Think of a tennis ball coming off a swinging racquet. In the film, Cooper plans to use a neutron star.
Leg 2 of 4

Cross the disk: could its glow kill you?

Gargantua is wrapped in a disk of gas spiralling into it. A busy disk would cook you with X-rays; the film’s disk is deliberately quiet, and the Ranger dives in over it, never through it.

  1. The gas orbits as it spirals in. Inner rings race faster than outer ones, so they rub, and that friction turns the energy of the fall into heat. Falling into a black hole releases many times more energy than nuclear fusion does.
  2. Hot things glow, and the hotter they are, the more energy each particle of light (photon) carries. A stove ring glows red, the Sun’s surface white, and gas at millions of degrees shines in X-rays: watch the heat bar.
  3. How hot the disk gets depends on how much gas is pouring in. A busy disk reaches millions of degrees and floods the space around it with X-rays, enough to kill the crew and fry the ship’s electronics, even through the hull.
  4. Kip Thorne, the film’s science adviser, gave Gargantua an “anemic” disk: only a trickle of gas, about as hot as the Sun’s surface. It shines in ordinary light rather than X-rays, so the crew can survive close to it.
Leg 3 of 4

Get close: would gravity tear you apart?

What hurts is the difference in pull between your head and feet. Small black holes stretch you; giant ones barely notice you.

  1. Gravity weakens with distance, so your feet, being closer, are pulled a bit harder than your head.
  2. Falling, you don’t feel the pull itself: every part of you falls together. You only feel that difference, as a stretch.
  3. The difference depends on how big you are compared with your distance from the centre. At a 10-Sun black hole the edge is 30 km out, so your 2 m is a big slice of it: huge stretch.
  4. Gargantua’s edge is 300 million km out. Your 2 m is nothing by comparison, so head and feet feel the same pull.
Leg 4 of 4

Stay there: why the planet never falls in

An orbit is a fall that never ends. Miller’s planet, and the Endurance parked in a higher orbit, stay up with no engines running.

  1. Gravity pulls the planet straight towards Gargantua all the time, so it is always falling (orange). Nothing holds it up.
  2. It is also racing sideways (teal). Without gravity it would carry on in a straight line, and a straight line drifts away from a circle: watch the distance gauge rise.
  3. Gargantua is round, so as the planet moves sideways the “ground” beneath it curves away. At just the right sideways speed, the planet falls exactly as far as the ground has curved away, and the gauge drops back to the same height.
  4. The animation splits this into steps so you can see it. In reality both happen at every instant, the steps shrink to nothing, and the path becomes a smooth circle: falling forever, always missing. Any slower and it would dip closer; any faster and it would swing out farther.
  5. Because everything falls together, nobody aboard feels a thing, just as astronauts float on the Space Station. That’s how the Endurance can wait in orbit while Romilly ages 23 years, without burning any fuel.

Key to the light paths

  • Direct. Near side of the disk, almost a straight line to you.
  • Bent over the top. Far side of the disk, top surface. This is the arch above the hole.
  • Bent underneath. Far side of the disk, bottom surface. This is the thinner arch below.
  • Wrapped around. Light that circled the hole before escaping: the thin photon ring.
  • Falls in. Look in these directions and nothing comes back. That is the black shadow.
  • Dashed circle. The event horizon at its true size. Bending of light makes the black shadow around it about 2.6 times wider.
  • Time rings (time map). How fast a clock hovering there ticks, compared with yours far away.
